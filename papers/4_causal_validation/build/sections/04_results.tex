\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Direction ablation is foundation-specific \label{causal-validation}}{Direction ablation is foundation-specific }}\label{direction-ablation-is-foundation-specific}

Ablating a foundation's direction from the residual stream specifically reduces log-probability of that foundation's continuations while leaving other foundations largely unaffected (Table\textasciitilde{}\ref{tab:ablation}).
Specificity (on-target \(\Delta\) minus off-target \(\Delta\)) is negative for all 18 (foundation \(\times\) layer) combinations, confirming that each direction carries information specific to its foundation.

\textbf{Layer dependence.} Specificity increases with depth.
At layer 4, mean specificity across foundations is \(-0.30\); at layer 8 it is \(-0.43\); at layer 12 it is \(-0.93\).
The strongest effects are sanctity at layer 12 (specificity \(= -1.89\), on-target \(\Delta = -2.03\) nats) and loyalty at layer 12 (\(-1.08\)).
This pattern---ablation most damaging in later layers---is consistent with the model relying more heavily on moral representations as they approach the output.

\textbf{Foundation heterogeneity.} Sanctity and loyalty directions are the most causally load-bearing (largest specificity at layer 12), while fairness and authority show moderate effects.
Care shows strong on-target effects but also larger off-target perturbation, consistent with care/harm co-activating with other moral content (the ``care saturation'\,' phenomenon observed in behavioral benchmarking, \S\ref{behavioral-grounding}).

\subsection{Steering injection shows dose--response specificity}\label{steering-injection-shows-doseresponse-specificity}

At low amplitude (\(\alpha = 1\)), injecting a foundation direction into the residual stream at layer 8 produces a \emph{positive} on-target boost (mean \(+0.20\) nats across foundations) with near-zero off-target effect (mean \(+0.03\)), yielding positive specificity for all six foundations (Table\textasciitilde{}\ref{tab:injection}).

\textbf{Dose--response curve.} As \(\alpha\) increases:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\alpha = 1\)--\(2\): specific boost. On-target log-probability increases, off-target is near baseline. This is the ``therapeutic window.'\,'
\item
  \(\alpha = 5\): mixed. Some foundations maintain positive on-target (sanctity \(+0.25\), loyalty \(+0.07\)), others turn negative. Specificity remains positive for all.
\item
  \(\alpha = 10\)--\(20\): non-specific degradation. Both on-target and off-target log-probabilities decrease, but off-target decreases \emph{more}, so specificity remains positive (\(+2.4\) at \(\alpha = 10\), layer 8). The model is pushed out of its functional range.
\end{itemize}

This dose--response pattern distinguishes the moral directions from random directions: noise injection would produce monotonic degradation at all amplitudes, not the low-\(\alpha\) specific boost we observe.

\textbf{Layer dependence.} Layer 12 maintains specificity to higher \(\alpha\) values than layers 4 and 8.
At \(\alpha = 5\), all six foundations show positive on-target \(\Delta\) at layer 12 (mean \(+0.25\)), while layers 4 and 8 are already showing degradation for some foundations.
This robustness at layer 12 parallels the strong ablation effects, confirming that later layers are the primary locus of moral direction utilization.

\subsubsection{4.3.1 Test set performance}\label{test-set-performance}

Per-foundation accuracy is uneven: care (87.5\%), sanctity (75.0\%), loyalty and authority (72.5\%) perform well, while fairness (45.0\%) and liberty (50.0\%) are weaker.
The confusion matrix reveals that fairness and liberty errors scatter across multiple foundations rather than concentrating on a single confound, suggesting these foundations have more distributed representations that share variance with multiple other directions.

\subsubsection{4.3.2 External validation: Moral Foundations Vignettes}\label{external-validation-moral-foundations-vignettes}

The MFV accuracy of 26.7\% (above the 16.7\% chance baseline) masks a systematic pattern: the confusion matrix shows that care/harm dominates classification regardless of the true foundation.
All five care items are correctly classified, but 23 of 25 non-care items are also classified as care.

This is not a failure of the directions but a property of the stimuli.
The MFV items are descriptions of witnessing morally transgressive acts (\texttt{You\ see\ a\ boy\ kicking\ a\ puppy\textquotesingle{}\textquotesingle{}),\ and\ witnessing\ harm\ *genuinely\ activates*\ the\ care/harm\ representation\ regardless\ of\ which\ foundation\ the\ scenario\ is\ designed\ to\ target.\ A\ fairness\ violation\ (}You see a judge giving a lighter sentence to a wealthy defendant'\,`) also involves potential harm to the disadvantaged party.
An authority violation (``You see a student cursing at a teacher'\,') also activates interpersonal harm sensitivity.

This co-activation is consistent with the integration geometry: the shared moral-salience component (mean pairwise cosine \(\sim 0.3\) between foundation directions) means that stimuli activating \emph{any} foundation will partially activate \emph{all} foundations, with care receiving disproportionate activation due to the harm content present in most moral scenarios.
Debiasing (subtracting the mean projection) does not resolve this because the care co-activation is genuine, not an artifact of the shared component.

\subsubsection{4.3.3 Causal prompt performance}\label{causal-prompt-performance}

The 85.4\% accuracy on causal evaluation prompts---which were designed for targeted foundation activation, not harm witnessing---confirms that the directions are behaviorally predictive when stimuli are foundation-specific.
Per-foundation accuracy is uniformly high (75--87.5\%), with no care dominance.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{SAE features partially recover moral subspace \label{sae-analysis}}{SAE features partially recover moral subspace }}\label{sae-features-partially-recover-moral-subspace}

\subsubsection{4.4.1 Training results}\label{training-results}

The layer-8 SAE achieves L0 = 1,932 (11.8\% of 16,384 features active per token) and FVU = 0.285 (71.5\% variance explained) after 3 epochs on 2M tokens.
The moderate FVU reflects limited training scale; production SAEs typically train on 100M+ tokens.

\subsubsection{4.4.2 Moral selectivity}\label{moral-selectivity}

Only 1 of 16,384 features has moral selectivity \(|s| > 0.1\) (mean activation on moral minus neutral text).
Moral information is distributed across many features rather than concentrated in a few---consistent with the high effective dimensionality of moral representations found in \citet{reblitzrichardson2026geometry}.

\subsubsection{4.4.3 Subspace overlap}\label{subspace-overlap}

The top-100 morally selective SAE features, despite individually showing no alignment with probe directions (\(0\%\) of features with \(|\cos| > 0.2\)), collectively span a subspace that captures 19.6\% of mean-difference direction variance.
The random baseline is \(4.89\% \pm 0.36\%\) (matching the analytical expectation of \(100/2048 = 4.88\%\)), yielding a ratio of \(4.01\times\) random (\(p < 0.001\) by permutation).
Probe-weight directions show weaker overlap (\(9.1\%\), \({\sim}2\times\) random), consistent with LEACE and probe-weight directions capturing partially different aspects of the moral subspace.

This result has two interpretations.
First, even a partially trained SAE discovers features that overlap with supervised moral directions, suggesting these directions reflect genuine structure in the model's representations rather than probing artifacts.
Second, the overlap is modest (19.6\%), indicating that either (a) the SAE needs substantially more training to decompose moral features cleanly, or (b) moral representations are inherently distributed across many low-selectivity features that resist separation by current SAE methods.
